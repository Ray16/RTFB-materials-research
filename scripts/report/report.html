01 / The design problem

Attaching a molecule is only the beginning.

The target is a polymer with molecules that can repeatedly accept and release electrons. A useful material needs suitable voltages, enough stored charge, accessible sites, and chemical durability.

Start with the formed polymer

Merrifield-type polymer: a polystyrene backbone carrying reactive benzyl-chloride attachment sites.

Attach a charge-storing group

Test a specific precursor and bond-forming reaction. A plausible drawing alone does not establish a workable route.

Test the occupied polymer

Check how site spacing, nearby groups and the polymer environment affect the result.

Three different models, three different questions. A free molecule tests the redox core; a small capped model tests a local attachment site; a short polymer segment tests neighboring sites. Results do not transfer automatically between them.

Can both electrons be used?

Both reduction steps must fit inside the electrolyte’s usable voltage range. A two-step ladder alone does not prove that they do.

The same ladder estimates whether two singly reduced molecules prefer to exchange an electron. That resistance is overestimated by about 23.4 kJ/mol in the ten-case spacing benchmark.

This tests one chemical process, not overall lifetime or resistance to decomposition.

How much must the molecule rearrange?

Reorganization energy, written λ, measures the energy cost of rearranging around electron transfer. It is one contributor to transfer speed.

The implementation agrees with 322 external calculations to a median absolute difference of 0.09 eV at the external method’s settings.

This is agreement between calculations, not measured kinetic accuracy. Production settings show a separate −0.24-eV offset; failed structure checks leave λ unqualified.

Calculation scope and quality checks

Primary molecular energies use ωB97M-V/def2-TZVPD with the SMD solution model in MeCN, on r²SCAN-D4 structures. The checked molecular cases use their declared nuclear terms and matched ferrocene reference. The four finite polymer cycles below use a separate r²SCAN/SVP protocol.

Each charged state must retain the intended bonds and remain electronically bound. Passing these checks is necessary, but does not establish reversible chemistry. Benchmark nuclear terms and broader production qualification retain the limitations in the publication record. [7]

04 / How much charge fits in the material?

More active sites help. Added mass works against them.

Capacity means stored charge per gram. It depends on the number of usable electrons and the mass counted—not just on the isolated redox core.

Two independent choices

Handle density: how many attachment sites the starting polymer has.

Conversion: how many of those sites actually receive a charge-storing group.

Neither is established experimentally here. Remaining benzyl-chloride sites are chemically different from styrene units with no handle.

An upper bound, not delivered capacity

The PMDI figure compares one versus two usable electrons per graft. The two-electron curves reach roughly 150 mAh/g at high loading under the plotted accounting assumptions.

Real accessible capacity can be lower. Added counterions, the chosen mass denominator, swelling and inaccessible sites all matter. Salt-mass scenarios must be kept separate. [1]

{{figure:pmdi_capacity}}
Design implication: evaluate electron count, attachment density and mass together. Do not substitute theoretical charge per gram for measured battery energy or cycling performance.
05 / Can we attach it?

The reaction conditions change the answer.

The modeled route attaches groups after the polymer has formed. Favorable calculated reaction energy supports a specified reaction; it does not establish reaction speed, selectivity or yield.

06 / What changes when sites fill up?

A nearby group can help—or hinder.

In four tested viologen polymer arrangements, the combined effect of occupying two sites differs from the sum of occupying them separately. The sign depends on the arrangement. [5]

Extra effect of occupying both sites

Negative: jointly occupied sites are more favorable than the additive expectation. Positive: less favorable. Units: kJ/mol. “One spacer” separates the occupied sites along the chain.

The key finding: a frozen pair of small capped groups gives only repulsive values (+7.6 to +9.6 kJ/mol) across these four configurations. That local-pair model does not reproduce the sign changes in the whole-chain calculation.

Why more structural sampling is still needed

The pinned sampling audit contains 100 checked electronic and 31 checked native inputs out of 240 planned slots. In the first complete 20-start occupied phthalimide control, expanding from 10 to 20 starts changed the averaged free energy by −2.40 kJ/mol—outside the 2-kJ/mol tolerance. This separate control does not provide an error bar for the four viologen values. [6]

07 / The next decisions

What would turn these results into a material choice?

Choose the next work by the uncertainty it resolves—not by the most attractive single number.

FIRSTComplete exact-candidate evidence

Fill missing voltage, charge-storage, structural and electron-transfer checks for designs with supported routes.

THENResolve the occupied polymer

Complete structural sampling and ion-association comparisons before extending finite-model findings to average polymer behavior.

EXPERIMENTALLYMeasure the material

Establish attachment conversion, swelling, accessible charge, transport and repeated-cycle durability.

Single-molecule calculations do not establish swelling, membrane crossover, site accessibility, conductivity or cycle life. No full material approval or experimental performance claim is made here.
